**Definition**: A Gaussian process ($\mathcal{GP}$) is a collection of random variables, any finite number of which have (consistent) joint Gaussian distributions.

A $\mathcal{GP}$ is a stochastic process fully specified by a mean function $m(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})]$ and a positive definite covariance function $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')=\text{cov}[f(\mathbf{x}), f(\mathbf{x}')]$.

Therefore, a $\mathcal{GP}$ is a generalization of a multivariate Gaussian distribution to infinitely many variables.

-  $m(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})]$ determines the mean of any arbitrary point $\mathbf{x}$ in the input space
	- Commonly assumed to be a zero-value vector, i.e. $m(\mathbf{x}) = 0$
- $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \text{cov}[f(\mathbf{x}), f(\mathbf{x}')]$ determines how any two points in the input space covary kernels
	- Specifies basic aspects of the process such as smoothness, periodicity, stationarity and isotropy

## Covariance functions
Most common choice is the squared exponential (SE)
$$
k_{\text{SE}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')=\exp \left( -\sum_{d=1}^{D}\quad \frac{(x_d-x'_d)^{2}}{2l^{2}} \,  \right) 
$$
- Also called Gaussian kernel, RBF kernel, exponential quadratic
- Parameter $l$ defining the characteristic length-scale
- Goes to unity as $\mathbf{x}$ becomes closer to $\mathbf{x}'$
- Nearby points are more likely to covary
- $\mathcal{GP}$ prior with a SE covariance function prefers smooth functions


## Constructing a $\mathcal{GP}$
- Given a dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{ \mathbf{x}_n,y_n \}_{n=1}^N$
- Define a $\mathcal{GP}$ prior for function values $\mathbf{f}:\mathbf{f}\sim \mathcal{GP}(m(\mathbf{x})=0, k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}'))$
- Build covariance matrix $\mathbf{K},$ where $\mathbf{K}_{ij}=k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)$
- Specifies a multivariate Gaussian distribution on $\mathbf{f}$
$$
\mathbf{f}\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{0,K})
$$
- This is our prior distribution over $\mathbf{f}$
- We can use it to sample from the $\mathcal{GP}$ prior

**Inference**
- For continuous outputs $y\in\mathbb{R}$ an obvious choice is a Gaussian likelihood
$$
p(y_n|f_n)=\mathcal{N}(y_n|f_n,\sigma^{2})
$$
- Using Bayes rule, we can compute the posterior over $\mathbf{f}$ (exact inference)
$$
p(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{y,X})= \frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f})}{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X})}
$$
**Marginal likelihood**
The marginal distribution of $\mathbf{y}$ is given by
$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X}) &= \int p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{X}) \, d\mathbf{f}  \\
&=\int \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}, \sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{O,K}) \, d\mathbf{f} \\
&=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{O}, \sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{K}) 
\end{aligned}
$$

We can use $p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X})$ to optimize the parameters $\theta$ of the covariance function
$$
\hat{\theta}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}\theta(\log(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{0}, \sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{K})))
$$

**Making predictions**
Given a new input $\mathbf{x}_*$, the joint distribution over $y_*,y_1,\dots,y_N$ is given by 
$$
p(y_*, \mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_*, \mathbf{X})=\mathcal{N}(y_*, \mathbf{y}|\mathbf{0,V})
$$
with 
$$
\mathbf{V}= \begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{K} & \mathbf{k}_* \\
\mathbf{k}_*^\top & \sigma^{2}+k_{**}\end{pmatrix}
$$
where $\mathbf{k}_*=k(\mathbf{x},x_*)$ and $k_{**}=k(x_*,x_*)$.

From the joint distribution, we can now determine the distribution of $y_*$, i.e. the predictive distribution:
$$
p(y_*|\mathbf{y,x}_*,\mathbf{X})=\mathcal{N}(y_*|\mathbf{k}_*^\top(\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{y},k_{**}+\sigma^{2}-\mathbf{k}_*^\top (\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{*})
$$
