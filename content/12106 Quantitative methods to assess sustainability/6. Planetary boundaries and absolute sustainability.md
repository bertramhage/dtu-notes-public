This material outlines the transition from relative eco-efficiency to Absolute Environmental Sustainability Assessment (AESA), a framework that evaluates whether human activities operate within Earth's biophysical limits using metrics like Planetary Boundaries, specific allocation methods, and Science-Based Targets.
### Moving from Efficiency to Effectiveness
Current sustainability efforts often rely on **eco-efficiency** (doing more with less) and relative assessments (is Product A better than Product B?). However, this approach is insufficient due to the **rebound effect**, where increased efficiency often drives higher consumption, negating environmental gains.
- We must shift from **relative sustainability** (improving efficiency) to **absolute sustainability** (ensuring impacts stay within the planet's biophysical limits).
- The goal is to move from asking "is it better?" to asking "is it good enough?".

### The Planetary Boundaries Framework
Absolute sustainability relies on a "top-down" approach defined by the **Planetary Boundaries framework**, which identifies nine Earth system processes (such as climate change and biosphere integrity) crucial for maintaining the stable conditions of the **Holocene** period.
- **Safe Operating Space:** The area below the boundary threshold (green zone) where humanity can safely operate. Crossing these boundaries increases the risk of irreversible environmental change.

### Assessment Methodology: AESA (Allocation and Calculation)
To perform an AESA, one must follow a structured process of quantifying limits and distributing them. This involves three specific steps outlined in the methodology slides:
- **Identify the Boundary:** You must first determine the global safe operating space. For climate change, the specific biophysical boundary used in the course exercise is **851.5 kg $CO_{2}e$/pers/year**.
- **Allocate the Space:** The global space is distributed to specific activities using distinct principles:
    - **Grandfathering (GF):** is an allocation principle that distributes the "Safe Operating Space" based on the status quo. It assumes that if an activity (like driving) is currently responsible for a large share of global pollution, it should be granted a proportionally large share of the future safe budget. For example, if a gas car accounts for 20% of current emissions, Grandfathering assigns that same 20% share of the safe operating space as the fixed allowance for all transport modes.
    - **Economic Allocation:** assigns a share of the safe operating space proportional to the money spent on an activity (eg. using Final Consumption Expenditure or LCC). This "utilitarian" approach assumes spending reflects value; for example, if mobility accounts for 15% of a person's annual spending, it is granted 15% of the safe emissions budget. Consequently, expensive activities are allocated a larger "right to pollute" than cheaper, potentially more essential ones.
- **Calculate the Ratio:** Finally, compare the activity's actual impact (derived from the Carbon Footprint Calculator/LCA) against its allocated space. This produces a **Sustainability Ratio**; a value greater than 1 indicates the activity exceeds its safe operating space and is unsustainable.
 $$SR = \frac{\text{Impact of Activity}}{\text{Allocated Safe Operating Space}}$$

### Practical Metrics: Footprints and Science-Based Targets
Two major applications of absolute sustainability in the real world are the Ecological Footprint and the Science-Based Targets initiative (SBTi).
- **Ecological Footprint:** Measures the land required to support consumption and sequester emissions. **Earth Overshoot Day** marks the date when humanity uses more resources than the planet can regenerate in a year.
- **Science-Based Targets (SBTi):** A corporate framework for setting emission reduction goals consistent with the Paris Agreement (1.5°C pathway). Companies must account for three scopes of emissions:
    - **Scope 1:** Direct emissions.
    - **Scope 2:** Indirect emissions from purchased energy.
    - **Scope 3:** Value chain emissions (often the hardest to measure but critical for total impact).
