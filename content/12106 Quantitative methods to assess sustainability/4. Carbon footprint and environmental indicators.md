### The Causal Framework: DPSIR Model
The carbon footprint sits between **pressure and state**: it quantifies emission pressure, translated into climate impact using the Global Warming Potential to reflect effects on radiative forcing.

To consistently quantify sustainability and relate human activity to climate change, the course utilizes the **DPSIR** framework (Driver, Pressure, State, Impact, Response). This model categorizes indicators along the cause-effect chain:
- **Drivers:** Human needs and activities, such as transportation demands or "Sustainable Cities" goals.
- **Pressures:** The immediate environmental stress, specifically emissions flows like GHG emissions ($kgCO_2/d$).
- **State:** The physical changes in the environment, such as Radiative Forcing ($W/m^2$) or temperature increase.
- **Impact:** The ultimate effects on ecosystems and humans, such as Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALY) or loss of species.
- **Response:** Societal actions to mitigate issues, such as taxes on diesel.

### Standardization of Metrics: GWP100
To aggregate different greenhouse gases into a single readable metric, the course utilizes **Global Warming Potential (GWP100)** over a 100-year horizon, as defined by the IPCC. This allows distinct gases to be expressed as **$kgCO_2$ equivalent** ($kgCO_2e$) based on their radiative efficiency relative to carbon dioxide.

**Common Conversion Factors:**
- **Carbon Dioxide ($CO_2$):** 1 $kgCO_2e/kg$.
- **Methane (fossil):** 29.8 $kgCO_2e/kg$.
- **Nitrous Oxide ($N_2O$):** 273 $kgCO_2e/kg$.
- **Sulfur Hexafluoride ($SF_6$):** 24,300 $kgCO_2e/kg$ (highly potent).

### Methodology: Calculating a Carbon Footprint
The lectures define a strict 5-step process for calculating a carbon footprint:
1. **Define Functional Unit (FU):** Identify the basis of comparison (e.g., "1 vehicle km" rather than just "a car"). This must represent the service provided, not just a ratio.
2. **Collect Inventory Data:** Gather data on composition and consumption (inputs like steel, electricity, petrol) required per Functional Unit ($Q$).
3. **Find Emission Factors ($E$):** Locate database values (e.g., Ecoinvent) for GHG emissions per unit of input (e.g., $kgCO_2e$ per kg of steel).
4. **Calculate Balance:** Multiply the input amounts (step 2) by the emission factors (step 3) to determine the contribution of each input.
5. **Interpretation:** Sum the results to find the total footprint and analyze hotspots.
$$
\text{Carbon footprint per FU} = \sum_i \left(\frac{Q_i}{\text{FU}} \cdot E_i\right)
$$

### Case Study Insight: Gasoline vs. Electric Vehicles (EV)
The lectures use a comparative Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) of vehicles to demonstrate sensitivity analysis and "break-even" points.
- **Manufacturing vs. Use Phase:** Gasoline vehicles are dominated by the **use phase** (burning fuel), whereas EVs have a higher **manufacturing footprint** (due to battery production) but a much lower use phase (depending on electricity source).
- **The Electricity Grid Factor:** The sustainability of an EV is highly sensitive to the energy mix. An EV charged with **hard coal** electricity has a similar or higher impact than a gasoline vehicle. An EV charged with **wind or photovoltaic** energy has a significantly lower total footprint.
- **Break-even Analysis:** Because EVs start with a "carbon debt" from manufacturing, they must be driven a certain distance to become cleaner than gas cars. With the average EU electricity mix, the EV becomes beneficial after approximately **25,000 km**.
### Global Disparities
There is a distinct disconnect between the **Producers** and the **Receivers** of climate change impact.
- **Inducing Impact:** North America, Europe, and East Asia have the highest carbon footprints per capita.
- **Receiving Damage:** Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia suffer the highest health damages (DALY/capita) despite contributing the least to the problem.
