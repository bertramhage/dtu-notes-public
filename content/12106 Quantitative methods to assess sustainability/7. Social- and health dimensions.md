### Quantifying Health: From Deaths to Minutes
To assess social sustainability quantitatively, researchers use **Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs)** rather than just mortality rates. This metric combines Years of Life Lost ($YLL$) due to premature death and Years Lived with Disability ($YLD$).
- **The "Micro-DALY":** To make these metrics relatable to daily choices, DALYs are converted into "minutes of healthy life gained or lost." One micro-DALY roughly equals one minute of healthy life.
- **Impact Pathways:** Social Life Cycle Assessment (S-LCA) uses "impact pathways" to trace how an environmental inventory (e.g., $CO_2$ emissions) leads to social endpoints. For example, $CO_2$ causes ocean acidification, which leads to biodiversity loss, eventually impacting human food security and mental well-being.

### Diet is the Leading Global Risk Factor
Dietary risks are currently the number one health risk factor globally, causing approximately 11 million deaths annually—more than tobacco or air pollution.
- **The Health Nutritional Index (HENI):** Specific foods can be quantified by minutes lost or gained. For example, consuming a hot dog results in approx. **36 minutes of healthy life lost** (due to processed meat, sodium, and trans fats), whereas nuts and fruits provide minute gains.
- **Coffee Case Study:** Drinking 3–4 cups of coffee per day is associated with a ~17% reduction in all-cause mortality. This direct health benefit (~22 minutes gained per cup) outweighs the negative environmental health impacts of producing the coffee (approx. 0.2 minutes lost) by a factor of roughly 100.

### Mobility: Physical Activity Outweighs Pollution Risks
When comparing transportation modes, the health benefits of physical activity (from biking or walking) generally dwarf the negative health impacts of exposure to air pollution.
- **Net Benefit:** Biking provides a gain of approx. 1.3 to 2 minutes of healthy life per km driven due to exercise. This is significantly higher than the minutes lost due to pollution exposure, even when accounting for vehicle emissions.
- **The Tipping Point:** The benefits of active transport persist unless the cyclist is in a city with extreme pollution (e.g., >100 $\mu g/m^3$ of PM2.5), such as New Delhi.

### Global Trade Displaces Health Burdens
There is a significant decoupling between those who benefit from consumption and those who suffer the health consequences.
- **Exporting Mortality:** High consumption in Western nations induces pollution in manufacturing regions (primarily Asia). For instance, US consumption is estimated to generate approx. 460,000 deaths in Asia annually due to particulate matter associated with production.
- **Climate Inequity:** Regions contributing the least to climate change (e.g., Sub-Saharan Africa) suffer the highest health burdens (up to 200 minutes of life lost per person/day) due to climate impacts caused largely by Western emissions.

### Practical Example: The Coffee Case Study
Goal: Quantify the health impact of a single cup of coffee.
1. **Establish the Risk Reduction**
	Drinking ~3.5 cups of coffee daily is linked to a **17%** reduction in all-cause mortality. This means each individual cup reduces your daily mortality risk by about **4.9%** ($17\% \div 3.5$).

2. **Determine the Background Burden**
	Determine the baseline health loss for the population using *Global Burden of Disease* data: In Denmark, the background burden is 30,900 DALYs per 100,000 people. Convert this annual figure into minutes lost per person per day:
$$0.309 \text{ days lost/day} \times 1440 \text{ min/day} = \mathbf{445 \text{ min of healthy life lost/day}}$$

3. **Calculate the Net Result**
	Multiply the daily burden by the risk reduction per cup to find the minutes gained:
$$445 \text{ min (daily burden)} \times 4.9\% \text{ (risk reduction)} = \mathbf{21.8 \text{ min gained per cup}}$$
**Conclusion**
The ~22 minutes of healthy life gained from consuming the coffee vastly outweighs the ~0.2 minutes lost due to the environmental pollution generated by producing it.
