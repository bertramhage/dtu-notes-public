### Expanding the Sustainability Scope
Sustainability assessments must move beyond solely minimizing environmental impacts to include **economic** and **social** dimensions. This holistic approach aids both producers (in product development) and consumers (in purchasing decisions).

### Macro-Economic Assessment Tools
The lectures introduce specific frameworks for analyzing broader economic systems before narrowing down to product-level costing:
- **Material Flow Analysis (MFA):** A quantitative analysis of process chains that measures the physical inputs and outputs (substances/materials) of processes at a specific point in time.
- **Input-Output (IO) Models:** These describe interdependencies between economic sectors using monetary values rather than physical flows. A key feature is the calculation of **Multipliers** to estimate the wider economic impact of a change in one sector:
    - **Direct Effect:** Impact of expenditures directly within the specific sector.
    - **Indirect Effect:** The impact that sector has on other industries in the supply chain.
	- **(Example):** Investing in a car factory creates jobs there (**direct effect**), but also boosts the steel and rubber industries that supply the factory (**indirect effect**), creating a total economic impact larger than the initial investment.

### Life Cycle Costing (LCC) Framework
The primary tool for assessing the economic pillar of sustainability is **Life Cycle Costing (LCC)**. Unlike simple purchase price analysis, LCC accounts for all costs incurred from the acquisition to the end-of-life of a product.

**The LCC Formula:**
The total Life Cycle Cost is calculated using the following summation :

$$LCC = C_{capital} + C_{operating} + C_{maintenance} + C_{disposal} - V_{residual}$$
where:
- **$C_{capital}$:** Initial investment or purchase price.
- **$C_{operating}$:** Recurring expenses to use the asset (e.g., fuel, electricity).
- **$C_{maintenance}$:** Costs to maintain the asset (e.g., tires, service).
- **$C_{disposal}$:** Costs to discard the asset (e.g., waste treatment).
- **$V_{residual}$:** The remaining value of the asset at the end of its useful life (subtracted from costs).

**Annual Depreciation:** Calculated as $\frac{Capital\ cost - Residual\ value}{Useful\ lifetime}$, reflecting how much value is lost over time.

### Application: Functional Units and Social Equity
To accurately compare products (e.g., Gasoline vs. Electric Vehicles), costs must be normalized to a **Functional Unit (FU)**, such as "1 vehicle km" over a specific lifespan (e.g., 150,000 km).

**Key Socio-economic Takeaway:**
While LCC may prove that technologies like EVs are cheaper over their lifetime due to lower operating costs, high capital costs create barriers to entry.
- **Sam Vimes "Boots" Theory:** This concept illustrates socioeconomic unfairness where lower-income households are forced to buy cheaper, inefficient products that cost more in the long run because they cannot afford the high upfront cost of efficient, durable alternatives.
- **Mitigation:** Mechanisms like leasing models can be analyzed via LCC to see if they lower barriers for low-income users.
