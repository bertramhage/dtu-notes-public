## Principal component analysis PCA
Takes advantage of the correlations between $p$ variables by utilizing a set of $q$ *eigenvectors* where typically $q\ll p$.

A fundamental concept is *variance* of the dataset.

The *covariance* matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ of data $\mathbf{X}$ is a $p \times p$ symmetric matrix where
- Off diagonal elements corresponds to the covariance between different variables $cov(X_1,X_2)$
- Diagonal elements corresponds to the variance of a particular variable $var(X_1)$

The trace of $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ gives the total variability of the data.

>[!info] Singular value decomposition
>Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) expresses a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ as 
>$$
>A = U \Sigma V^\top,
>$$
>where:
>- $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ contains the **orthonormal eigenvectors of $AA^\top$** (columns), ordered by descending singular values.  
>- $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is diagonal with the **singular values** (square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^\top A$ or $AA^\top$) on the diagonal.  
>- $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ contains the **orthonormal eigenvectors of $A^\top A$** (columns), also ordered by descending singular values.  

Assume $\mathbf{X}$ is centered around 0 ($\mathbf{X}:=\mathbf{X}-\bar{\mathbf{X}}$)
To obtain the optimal linear projection under which the variance is maximised
- Perform the eigendecomposition of $cov(\mathbf{X})$
- Order eigenvalues as $\lambda_1>\lambda_2>\dots>\lambda_p$
- Choose the first $q$ eigenvectors $\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2,\dots,\mathbf{e}_q$ corresponding to the $q$ largest eigenvalues

Formally the principal components matrix $\mathbf{Z}$ is given by
$$
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{V},
$$
where $\mathbf{V}$ are the eigenvectors of $\mathbf{\Sigma}$:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{\Sigma}&=\frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} \\
\mathbf{\Sigma V}&=\mathbf{\underbrace{ V }_{ \text{eigenvectors} }\cdot \underbrace{ \Lambda }_{ \text{eigenvalues} }}
\end{align}
$$
The combined percentage of variance explain in the first $r$ principal components are
$$
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{r} \lambda_k \, }{\sum_{k=1}^{p} \lambda_k \, },
$$
where $\lambda_k$ is the $k$'th eigen value of the covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ $\left(\sum_k\lambda_k=\sum_k\sigma^{2}_k=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\right)$

We might reconstruct the 0-centered data matrix with some loss, $\mathbf{\hat{X}}$:
$$
\mathbf{\hat{X}}=\mathbf{Z}_q\mathbf{V_q}^\top.
$$
## Multidimensional scaling ($\text{MDS}$)

Fundamental idea:
- At the original dimension $p$, each pair of objects $i \text{ and } j$ has a certain distance $d_{i,j}$
- At the lower dimension the same pair of objects has the **same** (or approximate) distance

- Useful for clustering and visualisation
- Common for exploratory stage
