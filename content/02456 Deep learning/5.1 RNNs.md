Used for **sequence data**, ex:
- Speech recognition, music generation, sentiment classification, DNA sequence analysis, machine translation, etc.

Formalise:
- **Input**: $\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x_1, \dots,x_T})$, where $\mathbf{x_t}$ can be a vector
- **Output**: $\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{y_1,\dots,y_T})$ and $\mathbf{y_t}$ can be a vector
(Length $T$ may vary between *datapoints*)

![[Pasted image 20250929132024.png|400]]
$\mathbf{h}_t=\sigma_1(W^{(1)}\mathbf{x}_t+W^{(\rightarrow)}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}+\mathbf{b}^{(1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(\rightarrow)})$
$\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t=\sigma_2(W^{(2)}\mathbf{h}_t+\mathbf{b}^{(2)})$
where $\sigma_1 \text{ and } \sigma_2$ are activations functions.


Can be stacked:
$\mathbf{h}^{(1)}_t=\sigma_1(W^{(1)}\mathbf{x}_t+W^{(\rightarrow1)}\mathbf{h}^{(1)}_{t-1}+\mathbf{b}^{(1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(\rightarrow1)})$
$\dots$
$\mathbf{h}^{(n)}_t=\sigma_n(W^{(n)}\mathbf{h}^{(n-1)}+W^{(\rightarrow n)}\mathbf{h}^{(n)}_{t-1}+\mathbf{b}^{(n)}+\mathbf{b}^{(\rightarrow n)})$
$\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t=\sigma_{n+1}(W^{(n+1)}\mathbf{h}^{(n)}_t+\mathbf{b}^{(n+1)})$

## Loss function
Like usual, with maximum likelihood estimation:
$\hat{\phi}= \operatorname*{arg\,max}_\phi \sum_i\log p(y^{(i)}|f_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}))$
where $\hat{y}^{(i)}=f_\phi(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})$ is the output of an RNN.

For an input-output pair $\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_T)$ and $\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{y}_1,\dots,\mathbf{y}_T)$ we usually assume
$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\mathbf{y}|f_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}))&=\prod_{t=1}^{T} p(\mathbf{y}_t|f_\phi(\mathbf{x})_t) \,  \\
&=\prod_{t=1}^{T} p(\mathbf{y}_t|f_\phi(\mathbf{x}_{\le t})_t) \, 
\end{aligned}
$$

## Vanishing and exploding gradients
Since
$$
h_t=Wh_{t-1}\implies h_t=W^th_0
$$
if we do an eigenvalue decomposition $W=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ we have
$$
h_t=Q\Lambda ^\top Q^\top h_0
$$
$\implies$ eigenvalues $<1$ decay to zero, eigenvalues $>1$ explode: Causes same behaviour of gradients.

**LSTM**: Architectures like LSTM cells help preserve gradient flow while still capturing long-term dependencies.
